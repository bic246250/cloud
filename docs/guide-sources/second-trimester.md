## Quickening and first movements

- You should start to feel your baby move between 16 and 24 weeks, and if this
  is your first baby you might not feel movements until after 20 weeks; if you
  have not felt your baby move by 24 weeks you should tell your midwife — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/your-babys-movements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Movements can feel like a gentle swirling or fluttering, and become kicks and
  jerky movements later — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/your-babys-movements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- NHS week-by-week guidance says many women notice the first signs of their baby
  moving between 18 and 24 weeks, and describes them as bubbling, fluttering or
  rolling — https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/pregnancy/week-by-week-guide-to-pregnancy/2nd-trimester/week-17/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- NHS week-by-week guidance says you may start feeling movement around week 18,
  and that it feels like a bubbling or fluttering inside the belly — https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/pregnancy/week-by-week-guide-to-pregnancy/2nd-trimester/week-18/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Anatomy scan at 18-22 weeks

- The 20-week screening scan, also called the anomaly scan, is an ultrasound to
  look for 11 different conditions and to check the baby's growth; it is usually
  done between 18 and 21 weeks, and can be done up to 23 weeks in some cases — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/your-pregnancy-care/20-week-scan/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- The scan usually takes around 30 minutes, there is no known risk to you or your
  baby, and it may take longer if the baby is moving a lot or is not in the right
  position; the sonographer checks the bones, heart, brain, spinal cord, face,
  kidneys and tummy, plus the placenta and the blood flow in the uterus — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/your-pregnancy-care/20-week-scan/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- The scan cannot find all conditions, and if clear pictures are still difficult
  you will be offered another appointment before 23 weeks; results are given after
  all the checks, and a specialist referral follows if there are signs of a
  condition — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/your-pregnancy-care/20-week-scan/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- NHS week-by-week guidance says you will be offered an anomaly scan at around 18
  to 20 weeks — https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/pregnancy/week-by-week-guide-to-pregnancy/2nd-trimester/week-18/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Baby development by week

- NHS week-by-week guidance describes development at 17 weeks (baby around 12cm
  long from head to bottom, can move the eyes, reacts to loud noises, opens and
  closes the mouth) and at 18 weeks (baby around 14.2cm, hearing, feeling,
  swallowing and sucking reflexes developing) — https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/pregnancy/week-by-week-guide-to-pregnancy/2nd-trimester/week-17/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- NHS week-by-week guidance describes development at 18 weeks and confirms the
  anomaly scan is offered at around 18 to 20 weeks — https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/pregnancy/week-by-week-guide-to-pregnancy/2nd-trimester/week-18/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Back pain, round ligament pain and pelvic girdle pain

- Back pain is very common in pregnancy, especially in the early stages, because
  ligaments become softer and stretch to prepare for labour, which puts a strain on
  the joints of the lower back and pelvis; NHS advice includes bending your knees
  and keeping your back straight when you lift, avoiding lifting heavy objects,
  moving your feet when you turn, wearing flat shoes to evenly distribute your
  weight, and getting enough rest — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/back-pain/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- You can take paracetamol to ease back pain while pregnant unless your GP or
  midwife says not to; contact your GP or midwife urgently if back pain in the
  second or third trimester comes with a fever, bleeding from the vagina, pain
  when you pee, or pain in your sides under the ribs — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/back-pain/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- NHS week-by-week guidance lists pains on the side of the baby bump caused by the
  expanding womb ("round ligament pains") among 24 week symptoms — https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/pregnancy/week-by-week-guide-to-pregnancy/2nd-trimester/week-24/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Pelvic pain in pregnancy is sometimes called pregnancy-related pelvic girdle pain
  (PGP) or symphysis pubis dysfunction (SPD); it is caused by stiffness of the
  pelvic joints or the joints moving unevenly at the back or front of the pelvis,
  and pain may be felt over the pubic bone, across the lower back, in the perineum
  or spreading to the thighs; it is not harmful to the baby but can make it hard to
  get around — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/pelvic-pain/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Call your midwife or GP if pelvic pain makes it hard to move around, hurts to
  get out of a car or turn over in bed, or is painful going up or down stairs;
  physiotherapy from a service that specialises in obstetric pelvic joint problems
  can relieve pain and improve pelvic joint position and stability — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/pelvic-pain/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- NHS week-by-week guidance says sudden sharp pains in the calves at night are
  probably cramp, common in pregnancy and caused by muscular spasms, and suggests
  rubbing the muscle hard or pulling your toes up towards your ankle, and
  exercising more in the day — https://www.nhs.uk/best-start-in-life/pregnancy/week-by-week-guide-to-pregnancy/2nd-trimester/week-20/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Glucose screening at 24-28 weeks

- Gestational diabetes is high blood sugar that develops during pregnancy and
  usually disappears after giving birth; it does not usually cause symptoms, so
  most cases are only found when blood sugar is tested during screening — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/gestational-diabetes/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- The screening test is an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), which takes about 2
  hours; it involves a blood test in the morning after not eating or drinking for
  8 to 10 hours, then a glucose drink, and it is done between 24 and 28 weeks — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/gestational-diabetes/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- If you have one or more risk factors, assessed at the booking appointment at
  around 8 to 12 weeks, you should be offered the screening test; gestational
  diabetes can mean the baby grows larger than usual, too much amniotic fluid,
  premature birth or pre-eclampsia, and the risks are reduced if it is detected
  early and managed — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/gestational-diabetes/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Whooping cough vaccination

- The whooping cough vaccine is always recommended if you are pregnant, is usually
  offered around 20 weeks, can be given from 16 weeks, and should be given before
  32 weeks to give the baby the best protection; it is recommended for every
  pregnancy even if you have had it before — https://www.nhs.uk/vaccinations/whooping-cough-vaccine/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Babies cannot have their first whooping cough vaccine until they are 8 weeks
  old, so vaccination in pregnancy passes protection to the baby through the
  placenta; when given during pregnancy the vaccine is around 90% effective at
  protecting babies from serious illness before their first vaccinations — https://www.nhs.uk/vaccinations/whooping-cough-vaccine/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- NHS guidance says there is no evidence that the whooping cough vaccine is unsafe
  for you or your unborn baby, and that the immunity you get passes to your baby
  through the placenta — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/whooping-cough-vaccination/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Blood group and anti-D

- Your blood group and rhesus (RhD) status are checked with a blood test at your
  first antenatal appointment at around 8 to 12 weeks; haemolytic disease of the
  fetus and newborn (HDFN, sometimes called rhesus disease) can happen when you
  are D negative and your baby is D positive — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/rhesus-disease-hdfn/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- If there is a risk of HDFN during pregnancy you will usually be offered
  injections to stop you developing antibodies — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/rhesus-disease-hdfn/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- People are either rhesus (RhD) positive or RhD negative; in the UK around 85% of
  people are RhD positive and around 15% RhD negative, and RhD status matters when
  an RhD negative woman is pregnant with a baby who is RhD positive — https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/-guidance-on-the-use-of-routine-antenatal-anti-d-prophylaxis-raadp-and-postnatal-care-for-rhd-negative-women/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- In pregnancy it is possible that the baby's blood cells could enter the mother's
  bloodstream in a feto-maternal haemorrhage (FMH), which generally happens just
  before birth and can also happen after a sensitising event such as a miscarriage
  or an amniocentesis; if the baby is RhD positive, antibodies may be produced
  against the D antigen, known as sensitisation — https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/-guidance-on-the-use-of-routine-antenatal-anti-d-prophylaxis-raadp-and-postnatal-care-for-rhd-negative-women/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Anti-D protects you from making antibodies against your baby's blood, and
  sensitisation becomes a problem during a future pregnancy rather than the
  current one — https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/-guidance-on-the-use-of-routine-antenatal-anti-d-prophylaxis-raadp-and-postnatal-care-for-rhd-negative-women/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Anti-D prophylaxis is usually given at 28 weeks and again after the birth,
  and it reduces the chance of sensitisation to 0.35% — https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/-guidance-on-the-use-of-routine-antenatal-anti-d-prophylaxis-raadp-and-postnatal-care-for-rhd-negative-women/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Warning symptoms

- A change in your baby's movements can be a sign of a problem, so it is important
  to get help if you notice any change; you should start to feel your baby move
  from around 16 to 24 weeks — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/common-symptoms/pregnancy-symptoms-you-need-to-get-help-for/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Call your midwife or maternity unit immediately if your baby is moving less than
  usual, you cannot feel your baby moving anymore, or there is a change to your
  baby's usual pattern of movements; do not wait until the next day — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/your-babys-movements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
