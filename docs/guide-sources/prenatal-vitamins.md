## Folic acid dose and neural tube defects

- The NHS recommends 400 micrograms of folic acid every day, ideally starting
  3 months before you try to get pregnant, or as soon as you find out you are
  pregnant, and continuing for the first 12 weeks; taking it for longer does not
  harm your baby — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Taking folic acid in this window reduces the chance of your baby having a
  neural tube defect (a problem with the brain or spine, or both) such as spina
  bifida, and can also help prevent other problems, such as cleft lip and palate
  or some heart conditions — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- The NHS recommends a higher dose of folic acid of 5 milligrams (5mg), which can
  be prescribed by a GP, if you are planning a pregnancy or are less than 12
  weeks pregnant and you or the baby's father has a neural tube defect, you or
  the baby's father has a relative with a neural tube defect, you have had a
  previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect, you have diabetes, you
  have a blood condition such as sickle cell anaemia or thalassaemia, or you
  take medicine for epilepsy or HIV — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- It is difficult to get the amount of folic acid you need from food alone — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- The word microgram is sometimes written as mcg or with the symbol μ followed
  by the letter g (μg) — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG lists folic acid among the vitamins and minerals needed during
  pregnancy — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG states that eating healthy foods and taking a prenatal vitamin every day
  should supply all the vitamins and minerals you need during pregnancy — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026

## Iron and anaemia screening in pregnancy

- Iron is needed to make red blood cells for you and your baby, and red blood
  cells carry oxygen around your body — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- When your body does not have enough iron, you may feel more tired than usual
  and you can have anaemia; your GP or midwife will recommend iron supplements — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Your iron levels are checked during routine blood tests at your 1st midwife
  appointment (booking appointment) and at around 28 weeks — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG lists 27 milligrams of iron a day as the recommended amount during
  pregnancy, to help red blood cells deliver oxygen to your fetus — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026
- Good sources of iron include red meat, beans, nuts, dried fruit, fortified
  breakfast cereals and soy bean flour, and you should avoid liver during
  pregnancy — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iron — fetched 22 October 2026
- Side effects of taking high doses (over 20mg) of iron include constipation,
  feeling sick, being sick and stomach pain — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iron — fetched 22 October 2026
- Taking 17mg or less a day of iron supplements is unlikely to cause any harm — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iron — fetched 22 October 2026

## DHA and omega-3 in pregnancy

- ACOG lists omega-3 fatty acids among the nutrients needed to support you and
  your fetus during pregnancy — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG names fatty fish such as salmon and sardines as sources of vitamin D — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026
- The NHS advises eating no more than 2 portions of oily fish a week, such as
  salmon, trout, mackerel or herring — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- You should eat no more than 2 tuna steaks (about 140g cooked or 170g raw) or
  4 medium-size cans of tuna (about 140g when drained) per week — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- You should avoid swordfish, marlin and shark — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- You should limit tuna because it has more mercury in it than other fish, and
  limit oily fish because they can have pollutants such as dioxins and
  polychlorinated biphenyls in them, which can be harmful to your unborn baby — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/foods-to-avoid/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Calcium and vitamin D

- Calcium helps build bones and keep teeth healthy — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/calcium/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Adults aged 19 to 64 and over need 700mg of calcium a day, and you should be
  able to get all the calcium you need from your daily diet — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/calcium/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG lists 1,300 milligrams of calcium a day for ages 14 to 18 and 1,000
  milligrams for ages 19 to 50 as the daily recommended amount during
  pregnancy — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026
- Taking high doses of calcium (more than 1,500mg a day) could lead to stomach
  pain and diarrhoea, and 1,500mg or less a day is unlikely to cause any harm — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/calcium/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Vitamin D helps regulate the amount of calcium and phosphate in the body,
  which is needed to keep bones, teeth and muscles healthy — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- If you're pregnant, it is recommended you take 10 micrograms of vitamin D
  every day between early October and late March, as we cannot make enough
  vitamin D from sunlight during this time — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Vitamin D is sometimes measured in international units (IU) and the
  recommended daily dose is 400 IU, the same as 10 micrograms — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Do not take more than 100 micrograms of vitamin D a day as it could be
  harmful — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- You may need to take a 10 microgram vitamin D supplement all year if you
  usually cover most of your skin when you're outside, if you spend a lot of
  time indoors, or if you have black or brown skin, as this affects how much
  vitamin D your skin makes — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG lists 600 international units of vitamin D a day as the daily
  recommended amount during pregnancy — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026
- Children from the age of 1 year and adults need 10 micrograms (mcg) of
  vitamin D a day, including pregnant and breastfeeding women — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Iodine and choline

- Iodine helps make thyroid hormones, which help keep cells and the metabolic
  rate healthy — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iodine/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Adults need 140 micrograms (μg) of iodine a day, and most people should be
  able to get all the iodine they need by eating a varied and balanced diet — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iodine/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Good food sources of iodine include cows' milk and dairy products, eggs, sea
  fish and shellfish — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iodine/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- If you follow a strict vegan diet and do not eat any fish, eggs, cows' milk
  or other dairy products, you may want to consider foods fortified with iodine
  or consider taking an iodine supplement, and some types of plant-based drinks
  (such as soya or oat) are fortified with iodine — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iodine/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Taking 0.5mg or less a day of iodine supplements is unlikely to cause any
  harm, and taking high doses of iodine for long periods could change the way
  your thyroid gland works — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iodine/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG lists 220 micrograms of iodine a day as the daily recommended amount
  during pregnancy, essential for healthy brain development — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG lists 450 milligrams of choline a day as the daily recommended amount
  during pregnancy, important for development of the fetus's brain and spinal
  cord, with best sources including milk, beef liver, eggs, peanuts and soy
  products — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026

## How to read a prenatal supplement label

- The word microgram is sometimes written as mcg or with the symbol μg — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Vitamin D is sometimes measured in international units (IU), and 10
  micrograms is the same as 400 IU — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Vitamin supplements can be bought from pharmacies, supermarkets and online,
  and if you need higher dose folic acid or other supplements you should speak
  to a GP — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- You may be able to get free vitamin supplements with Healthy Start — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026

## Vitamin A and retinol caution

- Do not take cod liver oil or supplements containing vitamin A (sometimes
  called retinol) when you're pregnant, as too much vitamin A can harm your
  baby's development, and always check the label — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- You should also avoid foods high in vitamin A, such as liver — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/pregnancy-vitamins-and-supplements/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Liver and liver products such as liver pâté are a particularly rich source of
  vitamin A, and if you're pregnant you should avoid eating liver or liver
  products — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-a/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Good sources of vitamin A (retinol) include cheese, eggs, oily fish, fortified
  low-fat spreads, milk and yoghurt — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-a/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- The amount of vitamin A adults aged 19 to 64 need is 700 μg a day for men and
  600 μg a day for women — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-a/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- You can get vitamin A by including good sources of beta-carotene in your
  diet, as the body can convert this into retinol; the main food sources are
  yellow, red and green (leafy) vegetables such as spinach, carrots, sweet
  potatoes and red peppers, and yellow fruit such as mango, papaya and
  apricots — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-a/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG lists 750 micrograms of vitamin A a day for ages 14 to 18 and 770
  micrograms for ages 19 to 50 as the daily recommended amount during
  pregnancy — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026

## Healthy diet and foods to focus on

- It's important to have a healthy, varied diet in pregnancy as this can help
  you get most of the vitamins and minerals you need, and it's best to get
  vitamins and minerals from the foods you eat — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/have-a-healthy-diet/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- You do not need to go on a special diet or "eat for 2", even if you are
  expecting twins or triplets — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/have-a-healthy-diet/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Dairy foods such as milk, cheese, fromage frais and yoghurt are important in
  pregnancy because they contain calcium and other nutrients — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/have-a-healthy-diet/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- Eggs produced under the Lion Code or the Laid in Britain scheme can be eaten
  raw or partially cooked by pregnant women — https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/keeping-well/have-a-healthy-diet/ — fetched 22 October 2026
- ACOG names milk, cheese, yogurt and sardines as best sources of calcium, and
  lists lean red meat, poultry, fish, dried beans and peas, iron-fortified
  cereals and prune juice as best sources of iron — https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/healthy-eating-during-pregnancy — fetched 22 October 2026
